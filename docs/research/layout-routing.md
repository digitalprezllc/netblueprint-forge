# Layout and routing for component / data-flow / threat-model diagrams: what the literature and the tools do

Research note, 2026-10-25. Written to settle how NetBlueprint Forge should place zones, nodes and services and route lines, after a run of visual reviews kept finding bends, congestion and near-overlaps that rule-by-rule patches fixed one at a time. The question asked: is there an established set of instructions, with precedence, for placement and routing that avoids overlaps and congestion? Short answer: yes, in three bodies of work: empirical aesthetics research, the layered ("Sugiyama") drawing pipeline used by every serious layout engine, and optimal orthogonal connector routing (visibility graph + A* + nudging). The renderer already followed the first two in spirit; the third it only approximated. Renderer 1.6.0 adopts it properly.

## 1. What makes a diagram readable (evidence, not taste)

Helen Purchase's experiments on human comprehension of node-link drawings are the standard reference. Ranked by measured effect on understanding:

1. **Edge crossings** matter most: reducing them has by far the largest effect.
2. **Bends** matter next: fewer bends, better comprehension.
3. **Symmetry** and uniform edge length have smaller or inconclusive effects.
4. Crossings at **right angles** hurt much less than shallow crossings; orthogonal drawings therefore tolerate crossings better than free-form ones.
5. **Shorter** edges read better than long detours.

For UML-style box diagrams specifically, later studies narrowed the list to "minimise crossings and bends; the rest is secondary". A 2026 replication on language models found the same two features dominate human accuracy. For a threat-model or network diagram the practical reading is: a straight line beats a short bent one, a bent line beats a crossing, and a right-angle crossing beats a line that touches or hugs something it does not connect to.

## 2. Placement: the layered pipeline

Every production layout engine that draws flows (Graphviz dot, Eclipse ELK Layered, yFiles Hierarchic, Dagre) runs Sugiyama's pipeline:

1. **Cycle breaking** (make the flow graph acyclic by reversing a few edges).
2. **Layer assignment** (each node gets a tier along the main axis; network simplex minimises total edge length).
3. **Crossing minimisation** (order the nodes inside each layer; the **barycentre** heuristic, each node placed at the average position of its neighbours in the adjacent layer, swept layer by layer, is the default in ELK and the one Sugiyama proposed; the median heuristic is the alternative).
4. **Node placement** (coordinates inside the layer; Brandes–Köpf aligns each node with one neighbour so that as many edges as possible are straight, then balances).
5. **Edge routing** (polyline, orthogonal or splines, with separate spacings for node–node, node–edge and edge–edge, within a layer and between layers; ELK defaults: 20 px node–node between layers, 10 px edge–node and edge–edge between layers).

Compound graphs (nodes inside nodes, i.e. our zones) are laid out recursively with padding per container; ELK can include children in one global layering when cross-hierarchy edges must stay straight. Spacing is specified per pair of element kinds and measured from the element's margin (which includes its labels), not from its border; edges are measured to their centre line.

**How NetBlueprint maps onto this.** The model's zone nesting fixes the containers and the declaration order fixes the layers and the order inside a row, so phases 1–3 are given by the author, with two automatic corrections: the Internet column is ordered by the barycentre of its partners, and the step path is locked onto one centre line by lifting rows (our Brandes–Köpf equivalent: straighten the edges of the primary path, then balance). What was missing was a principled phase 5.

## 3. Routing: optimal orthogonal connector routing

Wybrow, Marriott and Stuckey ("Orthogonal Connector Routing", Graph Drawing 2009; implemented in libavoid, used by Inkscape and Dunnart; extended in "Seeing Around Corners", Diagrams 2014) showed that the ad-hoc heuristics interactive editors use (Visio, OmniGraffle, Dia at the time) produce "strange routes and even routes that pass through other objects", and replaced them with a three-stage method that is optimal for a stated objective:

1. **Orthogonal visibility graph.** Take the *interesting points*: every corner of every obstacle's bounding box and every connector port. Draw the horizontal and vertical lines of sight through them; the graph's nodes are the intersections that are not inside an obstacle, its edges join each node to its nearest neighbour north, south, east and west when nothing lies between. Any valid orthogonal route can be shrunk onto this graph without getting longer or gaining a bend, so searching the graph loses nothing. (In practice a buffer is added around each obstacle so lines keep a minimum distance; libavoid's `shapeBufferDistance`, 4 px by default in ELK.)
2. **A\* search per connector** over states (node, direction of entry), minimising a monotonic penalty `f(length, bends)`: a straight continuation costs its length, a right-angle turn costs its length plus the bend penalty, and the heuristic adds the Manhattan distance plus a lower bound on the remaining bends (0–4, derived from the entry direction required at the destination). Ties are broken deterministically: straight before right before left, and the most recently extended path first, which gives "initially straighter" routes and predictable behaviour. The search can be extended with further penalties: libavoid exposes `segmentPenalty` (bends; ELK default 10), `crossingPenalty`, `clusterCrossingPenalty` (crossing a group boundary), `fixedSharedPathPenalty` (overlapping the fixed first or last segment of another connector), `portDirectionPenalty`, `reverseDirectionPenalty`, and nudging controls (`idealNudgingDistance`, 4). yFiles' orthogonal router has the same shape: fewest direction changes first, a grid option, a minimum edge-to-edge distance (and optionally a separate edge-to-node distance), a `crossingCost` where a value *n* means "rather bend *n* times than cross", recommended 1–3, and local crossing minimisation at node sides.
3. **Nudging / final placement.** Connectors that share a visibility edge (a bus) are ordered so that crossings happen only where a connector enters or leaves the shared stretch, never in the middle (the left/same/right insertion rule, Theorem 3 of the paper: minimal crossings, no added bends), then separated by a minimum distance; middle segments of Z and S shapes are moved to the **centre of their alley** when that costs nothing, and the final coordinates are found by projecting desired positions onto non-overlap constraints (VPSC). Users of Dunnart and Inkscape reported this step has a large effect on perceived quality.

Two things the literature is explicit about: **routing through a fine uniform grid is too slow and placement-sensitive**, which is why the visibility graph (a non-uniform grid tailored to the geometry) is used; and **Graphviz's `ortho` router cannot honour ports** and is documented as giving suboptimal routes when ports are specified, which is exactly our situation (every line has a side and a port).

## 4. Domain conventions for the diagrams we draw

- **Threat-model DFDs** (OWASP Threat Dragon, Microsoft TMT, STRIDE guides): trust boundaries are dashed containers; every element sits inside the boundary that applies to it; external actors are kept apart from internal processes; every flow is labelled with the data it carries, and every boundary crossing is marked, because that is where threats concentrate. Threat Dragon's diagrams are not hierarchical; ours are, which is why zone nesting and title rows matter so much for routing.
- **Azure Well-Architected guidance on design diagrams**: arrows always, from the initiating component to the dependency, never double-headed (show request/response as annotation or two flows); label every icon, container and relationship; standardised colours, line weights, arrowheads and border styles; accuracy over simplicity ("don't depict a PaaS service inside a subnet if it is reached over a private endpoint"); title, description, date, author, version on every diagram; a legend whenever line or border styles carry meaning; layer diagrams rather than overload one. All of this is already in the renderer (one-way arrows from the initiator, Title Case labels, signature block, generated legend, tabs).
- **Architecture-diagram practice**: flow left to right for data and time, top to bottom for tiers; group related components inside boundaries drawn as swimlanes or dashed borders; keep important paths short; avoid crossing lines; consistent white space; five to seven lanes at most; one question per diagram.

## 5. The precedence model (what wins when goals conflict)

Synthesised from the above and written into the renderer. Hard rules are never traded; soft rules are weighed in this order by the router's cost function.

| Rank | Rule | Where it lives |
| - | - | - |
| H1 | Boxes never overlap; a child sits inside its parent with padding; a bar keeps 12 px clear of anything outside its zone | layout (`gapX/gapY`, zone margins) |
| H2 | A line never passes through a box, a title row, a glyph corner or the seam labels, and keeps its distance from them; it may cross a zone border | visibility graph: all of these are obstacles with a buffer (12 px boxes and bars, 8 px text rows, 4 px glyph corners); only a port's own 12 px stub comes closer |
| H3 | Lines are orthogonal; each end leaves or enters through the side the port is on, straight, for at least 12 px | A\* start/goal direction constraints; grid lines at border + buffer |
| H4 | Parallel lines stay a lane apart (20 px where the side allows, 10 px on short sides such as a component row); two lines never cross a bar within 8 px of each other; a glyph on a line sits where no other line passes | port lanes in route-priority order; bar lanes; lane spreading; glyph placement |
| S1 | Fewest bends | bend penalty 70 px per turn |
| S2 | Fewest crossings | crossing penalty 45 px per crossing of an already-routed line |
| S3 | The primary (step) path is straight: level partners meet on one centre line, a route whose stops all face one axis gets both ends on one coordinate, a bar is crossed level with the port behind it | centre-line locking; the straightening pass; the first flow on a side keeps its lane; primary flows routed first; the bar look-ahead |
| S4 | Shortest Manhattan length | base cost |
| S5 | Stay out of zones that are neither endpoint's and off the flanks of boxes: route through the gutters between zones | foreign-zone surcharge 60 % per pixel; 30 % per pixel within 7 px of a node's or glyph corner's buffer |
| S6 | Centre in the alley, bend before a bar rather than after it, keep deterministic tie-breaks (straight, then right, then left) | alley centring for free middle segments; a same-lane overlap with another line costs a crossing per edge, so parallel lanes are preferred to shared ones; the look-ahead prefers the gutter side; neighbour order in A\* |

Author overrides stay available (`via`, `edge`, `row`, `order`); the rules decide everything the author did not.

## 6. What changed in the renderer (1.6.0) as a result

- **Routing engine replaced.** The Z/L-with-candidates heuristic became an orthogonal visibility-graph router: interesting coordinates from every obstacle edge pushed out by its buffer, every endpoint port with its stub (12 px) and U-turn lane (26 px), the zone gutters (16 px outside each zone), the canvas margins and a half-lane ladder across every bar; A\* over (node, heading) with the cost function of §5 (length, 70 per bend, 45 per crossing or same-lane overlap, 60 % inside a foreign zone, 30 % along a node's flank); primary flows routed first so later flows route around them; a straight L/Z fallback only when a port is boxed in (none of the three examples needs it).
- **Bars are crossed where the router decides.** Only endpoint ports are assigned before routing. A boundary bar is a *goal set*: every lane on the line a buffer outside its entry face that lies inside the bar's usable band, clear of its glyph stacks, not within 8 px of a line already through it (within a lane width costs about a bend). Each lane carries a look-ahead: what the rest of the route will pay for it, following a chain of bars to the endpoint behind them (level with that port: nothing; a lane its side can take: almost nothing, and the port then adopts the lane; otherwise the two bends of a jog, with a bias toward bending in the gutter *before* the bar; on the other axis, one bend when the lane sits on the port's approach side with a clear way, three otherwise). The bar's entry face is chosen when the leg is routed, from where the line actually comes from.
- **Ports are lanes with priority.** On each box side the story's flows come first (data plane before tunnels, auth, token and control); the first keeps its lane, a later one that would land within a lane width tries its alternatives, then asks the holder to move to one of its own, then takes the nearest free lane; lanes are 20 px apart, 10 px on short sides. A port aims at its real partner, looking through bars (the midpoint when both sides can reach it, the centre the shorter side can reach otherwise, an L-corner just past the partner's box when the partner's side runs the other way, the side's middle when the partner is out of reach), and an endpoint whose neighbour is a perpendicular bar aims at the bar's band.
- **Straightening pass.** A route whose two endpoints face along one axis and whose bars are all crossed straight becomes one line when a coordinate exists that both sides have room for and every bar accepts (each endpoint's own first, then 10 px steps either side).
- **Nudging kept and made safe.** One placement validator serves alley centring and lane spreading: a moved segment and its two connectors must stay a full buffer from every box (stubs excepted) and the connectors keep their direction and length. Lane spreading steps by a lane width, then half a lane. Bar labels are placed after routing.
- **Diagnostics.** `window.__nb` now also reports `nearMisses` (a segment within 8 px of a box it does not touch) and exposes the router's grid (`router.XS / YS / nodeOk / edgeOk / INFL`); the in-browser checker counts real bends per flow, crossings, same-lane overlaps and short jogs.
- **Placement unchanged.** Zone nesting, declaration order, the Internet-column barycentre and centre-line locking stay as they were; barycentre ordering inside zone rows was considered and not added (declaration order is the author's intent and the examples did not need it).

Measured on the three examples after the change (hard checks all zero: collisions, near misses, lines over text, glyph clashes, jogs under 20 px, shared stretches, fallbacks): Partner Access 6 crossings and at most 2 bends per flow; Admin Access 4 crossings, at most 4 bends; Entra Application Proxy 6 crossings, at most 2 bends; Azure hub-spoke 31 crossings (from 40 before the look-ahead) with the longest blocked path at 6 bends.
